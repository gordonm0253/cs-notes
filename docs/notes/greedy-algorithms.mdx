# Greedy Algorithms

## Introduction

Greedy algorithms build up a solution piece-by-piece, taking locally optimal choices at each step (without reconsidering past choices). For certain problems, locally optimal choices result in a globally optimal solution; in others, they produce a near-optimal one (see [approximation algorithms](./approximation-algorithms.mdx)). In this section, we explore greedy algorithms that produce an optimal solution, as well as two styles of correctness proofs for greedy algorithms: an exchange argument or a greedy stays ahead argument.

## Exchange Argument

Suppose that our greedy algorithm produces a solution $A$. Our goal is to prove that anything in our solution $A$ can be swapped with anything in the optimal solution $O$ without losing optimality.

1. Assume that your greedy solution and the optimal solution you choose differ in some way. (If this is not the case, then $O = A$ and we are done!)
2. Show that it is possible to make progress, that is “exchange” elements in $O$ to make it more similar to your solution $A$ while preserving the optimality of $O$.
3. Show that this exchange progress terminates, and when it does $O = A$ where $O$ is optimal. Conclude $A$ is optimal.

:::note
Make sure you exchange elements from $O$, not $A$! If you do this, then you change the solution that you trying to prove is optimal!
:::

## Minimum Spanning Tree

Suppose we have an connected, undirected graph $G = (V, E)$, where edge $e \in E$ has some positive edge weight $c_e$. We want to find the a subset of the edges $T \subseteq E$ such that the graph $(V, T)$ is still connected and the sum of edge costs $\sum_{e \in T} c_e$ is minimized. We call $T$ the minimum spanning tree of our graph $G$.
